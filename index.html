01 / Understand the idea

A little light. A little shadow.
A whole new feeling.

Neumorphism, often called soft UI, makes elements appear molded from their background. A light shadow and a dark shadow suggest depth while the element and its surroundings share a similar color.

01

A shared surface

The page and its components use related colors, so controls feel like part of one continuous material.

02

Light creates depth

Opposing light and dark shadows suggest a consistent light source. Their distance and softness shape the effect.

03

Interaction feels tangible

A raised button can settle inward when pressed. Small changes in depth help an interface respond to touch.

Soft surfaces. Clear communication.

Keep text, boundaries, and focus indicators easy to see. Shadows create the aesthetic; labels and visible states make it usable.

04 / Design with intention

Keep the softness.
Make the purpose clear.

Start with one component, such as a settings panel or a featured card. Give it depth where that depth helps someone understand what to do.

Experiment with a surface

  1. Give light a direction

    Keep the light shadow toward one corner and the dark shadow opposite it. A consistent light source makes surfaces feel related.

  2. Let important details stand out

    Use readable text, visible field boundaries, and clear keyboard focus. Never make shadow or color the only sign of a selected state.

  3. Leave room to breathe

    Separate components enough for their shadows to stay distinct. Use flatter surfaces for dense content and supporting information.

  4. Design for fingers and keyboards

    Make controls comfortable to tap, preserve native behavior, and check the layout on small screens as well as larger displays.

A few things to know

Curious? Good.

Here is where neumorphism can fit into your design process.

How is it different from skeuomorphism?

Skeuomorphism borrows the appearance of real objects, such as leather textures or physical dials. Neumorphism uses a more restrained treatment: similar surface colors and paired shadows that suggest raised or recessed shapes.

Should I use it across an entire website?

You can, but depth works best with a clear hierarchy. Start with a few controls or cards and leave ordinary text areas flat. Too many competing shadows can make an interface harder to scan.

Can a soft interface be accessible?

The visual style alone does not establish accessibility. Keep sufficient contrast, visible focus, persistent labels, and meaningful control states. Check keyboard operation, zoom, screen readers, and real devices as part of your implementation.

Do I need a framework or a design library?

No. Semantic HTML, CSS backgrounds and shadows, and a little JavaScript for interactive behavior are enough to build a showcase like this one.
